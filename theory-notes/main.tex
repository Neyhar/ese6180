\begin{center}
{\Large\textbf{Learning to Control When We Cannot See Everything}}\\[5pt]
{\large A guided story about history, action chunks, and stability}
\end{center}

Imagine controlling a moving object while seeing only its position. A single picture tells you where it is, but not which way it is moving. Two pictures may reveal its motion. Now imagine choosing several control actions at once and committing to them before looking again. Even a good plan can become outdated if something pushes the object after you make it.

Our project puts these two difficulties together: \emph{how much past information does the policy need, and how often should it revise its plan?} Linear systems let us separate the difficulties and explain them mathematically before introducing nonlinear dynamics.

These notes follow a sequence of questions. Each formal claim comes after its motivation and is followed by a proof. On a first reading, follow the examples and the paragraphs labeled \textbf{What this tells us}; return to the proofs afterward. The results are standard facts or elementary derivations for our setup, not claims of new general control theory. The final sections distinguish the analysis we can derive from computations still to be implemented.

\section{First, what can the expert see that the learner cannot?}
The \emph{state} is the collection of variables needed to predict the system's next step. For a moving object it might include position and velocity. The \emph{observation} is what the sensor actually reports. They need not be the same.
\[
\underbrace{x_{t+1}}_{\text{next state}}
=\underbrace{Ax_t}_{\text{natural evolution}}
+\underbrace{Bu_t}_{\text{effect of action}}
+\underbrace{w_t}_{\text{unexpected disturbance}},
\qquad
\underbrace{y_t=Cx_t}_{\text{sensor reading}}.
\]
The expert sees $x_t$ and chooses $u_t^*=K^*x_t$. The learner sees $y_t$ and its previous actions. Partial observability means that different states can produce the same sensor reading. The full state still has Markov dynamics; a single observation need not contain enough information for prediction or control.

Here is the notation we will use. All norms are Euclidean or their induced matrix norms unless a weighted norm is specified.
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{cl}
$n$ & Number of state coordinates: $x_t\in\R^n$.\\
$p$ & Number of observed coordinates: $y_t\in\R^p$.\\
$m$ & Number of action coordinates: $u_t\in\R^m$.\\
$r$ & History length: how many observations the learner retains.\\
$h$ & Observability index: a noiseless state-reconstruction threshold.\\
$H$ & Prediction horizon: how many actions the policy outputs.\\
$\ell$ & Execution length: how many outputs it applies before replanning.
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\subsection{A small example we can understand by hand}
Use the illustrative system
\[
A=\begin{bmatrix}.9&.2\\0&.8\end{bmatrix},\quad
B=\begin{bmatrix}0\\.2\end{bmatrix},\quad
C=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\end{bmatrix},\quad
K^*=\begin{bmatrix}-.5&-.6\end{bmatrix}.
\]
Only the first coordinate is visible. States $(1,1)$ and $(1,-1)$ both produce $y=1$, but the expert chooses $-1.1$ in the first state and $0.1$ in the second. A deterministic policy using only this observation cannot reproduce both decisions. This is an information problem before it is a learning problem.

Now assume no disturbances. Two observations reveal the hidden coordinate:
\[
y_t=.9y_{t-1}+.2x_{2,t-1}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
x_{2,t-1}=5(y_t-.9y_{t-1}).
\]
To find its \emph{current} value, we also account for the action we took:
\[
x_{2,t}=.8x_{2,t-1}+.2u_{t-1}.
\]
History supplies missing information, and the recorded action tells us how our own intervention changed the state. This example is for intuition; the dimension sweep uses a different, cyclic system family.

\section{So are we learning the expert matrix or something else?}
Our goal is to imitate the expert's decisions. But the learner's input is a history rather than the full state:
\[
z_t=\operatorname{col}(y_{t-r+1},\ldots,y_t,
                       u_{t-r+1},\ldots,u_{t-1}).
\]
There are $r$ observations and $r-1$ actions between them. Thus $z_t$ has $d_r=rp+(r-1)m$ entries. The action $u_t$ is not included: it is what we are about to choose.

For one action, the learner computes $\widehat u_t=\widehat W_0z_t$. We want
\[
\boxed{\widehat W_0z_t\approx K^*x_t,}
\]
not equality of the two matrices. Their input dimensions differ. If history reconstructs the state as $x_t=M_rz_t$, an ideal action map is $W_0^*=K^*M_r$. It means ``infer the state, then apply the expert,'' although regression can learn the combined map directly.

For a chunk, the output is a stack of $H$ actions:
\[
\begin{bmatrix}\widehat u_t\\\widehat u_{t+1}\\\vdots\\\widehat u_{t+H-1}\end{bmatrix}
=\begin{bmatrix}\widehat W_0\\\widehat W_1\\\vdots\\\widehat W_{H-1}\end{bmatrix}z_t
=\widehat Wz_t.
\]
The matrix has $Hm$ rows and $d_r$ columns, so it has $Hm d_r$ fitted numbers. This is \emph{parametric linear regression}. The simulator knows $A,B,C,K^*$; the regression algorithm receives only histories and expert action targets.

For example, the sweep case $n=10,h=4$ has $p=3$ sensors and $m=1$ action. With $r=4,H=16$, the input has $4\cdot3+3=15$ entries and $W$ has shape $16\times15$. The learner fits 240 numbers to predict 16 scalar actions.

\section{How much history would be enough in a perfect world?}
Temporarily remove disturbances and learning error. An initial state leaves a sequence of observable traces: $Cx$, then $CAx$, then $CA^2x$, and so on. Stack these traces into
\[
\mathcal O_r=\operatorname{col}(C,CA,\ldots,CA^{r-1}).
\]
If its columns are independent, no two different initial states have the same traces. The smallest such $r$ is the \emph{observability index} $h$. In our two-dimensional example $h=2$.

Actual observations also include the effects of past actions. Think of reconstruction as three steps: subtract those known effects, recover the state at the start of the window, and propagate it to the present.

\begin{theorem}[Enough noiseless history can recover the state]\label{thm:recon}
Assume $w_t=0$, exact observations, and $\rank\mathcal O_r=n$. There exists a linear map $M_r$ such that $x_t=M_rz_t$ for every trajectory, including trajectories driven by learner actions rather than expert actions.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Let $s=t-r+1$, and stack the observations and inputs as $Y,U$. Iterating the dynamics gives
\[
Y=\mathcal O_rx_s+\mathcal T_rU,\qquad
x_t=A^{r-1}x_s+\mathcal R_rU.
\]
Here $\mathcal T_r$ records how past actions affect the observations, and
$\mathcal R_r=[A^{r-2}B\ \cdots\ B]$ records their effect on the current state. More explicitly, block $(i,j)$ of $\mathcal T_r$ is $CA^{i-1-j}B$ for $j<i$, and zero otherwise, with indices starting at zero. Choose a left inverse $L_r\mathcal O_r=I$. Substitution gives
\[
x_t=A^{r-1}L_r(Y-\mathcal T_rU)+\mathcal R_rU,
\quad
M_r=[A^{r-1}L_r\quad\mathcal R_r-A^{r-1}L_r\mathcal T_r].
\]
For $r=1$, the input blocks are empty.
\end{proof}

\textbf{What this tells us.} The expert's behavior is representable by a history policy when $r\ge h$. It does not say regression will find the right map. It also does not say noisy histories reveal the state perfectly. If two states produce very similar traces, even small measurement errors can cause large reconstruction errors. Rank establishes uniqueness; conditioning describes sensitivity.

Nor is $h$ always the minimum history needed for an \emph{action}. If the expert ignores an invisible state coordinate, we may imitate its action without recovering that coordinate. For arbitrary fixed known inputs, the precise condition is
\[
\ker\mathcal O_r\subseteq\ker(K^*A^{r-1}).
\]
In words: every state difference invisible in the history must also be irrelevant to the current expert action. To verify this, consider two initial states differing by $v$. Their histories agree exactly when $\mathcal O_rv=0$, and their current actions agree exactly when $K^*A^{r-1}v=0$. If the inclusion holds, the target is a well-defined linear function of the observation traces. This is a weaker requirement than full state recovery.

\section{What does executing an action chunk actually change?}
Suppose $H=4$ and $\ell=2$. At time $b$, the policy predicts four actions. It applies the first at $b$, the second at $b+1$, and then replans at $b+2$ from the updated history. It discards the unused predictions. The two applied actions can differ: chunking does not mean holding one action constant.
\[
\begin{array}{c|cccc}
\text{Time}&b&b+1&b+2&b+3\\\hline
\text{Plan used}&\text{new plan}&\text{same plan}&\text{new plan}&\text{same plan}\\
\text{Action}&W_0z_b&W_1z_b&W_0z_{b+2}&W_1z_{b+2}
\end{array}
\]
The distinction is between retaining new measurements and \emph{using them to revise the current actions}. Our simulator retains measurements every step, but only revises the plan at boundaries.

Before claiming that delayed feedback hurts, ask what happens when prediction is perfect and nothing unexpected occurs. Write $F=A+BK^*$, the expert's closed-loop state transition. Its nominal future actions from state $x_b$ are $K^*x_b,K^*Fx_b,K^*F^2x_b,\ldots$.

\begin{proposition}[In the ideal noiseless case, execution lengths tie]\label{prop:ideal}
With exact reconstruction $x_b=M_rz_b$, choose $W_j^*=K^*F^jM_r$. Starting from a common consistent noiseless history, this policy follows the expert exactly for every $1\le\ell\le H$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
At the boundary the predicted action is $K^*x_b$. If $x_{b+j}=F^jx_b$, the next state under the committed action is
$AF^jx_b+BK^*F^jx_b=F^{j+1}x_b$.
Induction proves agreement throughout the chunk. Reconstruction is exact again at the next boundary, so the argument repeats.
\end{proof}

\textbf{What this tells us.} Observability alone cannot explain why one execution length beats another. We need disturbances, fitting error, or mismatch. Those ingredients are present in our experiments.

\section{Why can a policy fit demonstrations perfectly and still fail?}
Training pairs a history $z_i$ with an expert action chunk $a_i^*$. The fitted matrix minimizes
\[
\sum_i\norm{Wz_i-a_i^*}^2+\lambda\norm{W}_F^2.
\]
The first term rewards matching demonstrations. The second penalizes large weights. Neither directly asks whether the learner's own feedback system is stable.

A simple regression example explains the danger. Suppose every training input has the form $(a,a)$, and the target is $a$. Every predictor $c_1z_1+c_2z_2$ with $c_1+c_2=1$ fits perfectly. At the new input $(1,0)$, the prediction is $c_1$, which the training data did not determine. More examples on the same line cannot resolve this ambiguity. Ridge chooses among predictors, but does not reveal the missing behavior.

Our expert trajectories have an analogous restriction.
\begin{proposition}[Noiseless demonstrations occupy a restricted set]
Under the expert, $z_t=S_rx_{t-r+1}$, where $S_r$ stacks $CF^j$ for $0\le j<r$ and $K^*F^j$ for $0\le j<r-1$. Consequently, the history-feature rank is at most $n$. If $\Delta WS_r=0$, then $W$ and $W+\Delta W$ agree on every such expert history.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The expert evolves as $x_{s+j}=F^jx_s$. Substituting into every observation and action in the history gives the stated factorization. Its image has dimension at most $n$. On that image, the prediction difference is $\Delta WS_rx_s=0$.
\end{proof}

\textbf{What this tells us.} The learner can be accurate on the histories the expert visits yet unreliable on histories created by disturbances and its own actions. Failure is not inevitable, but small validation loss alone does not rule it out. Our $n=10,h=4,r=3$ case illustrates the distinction: validation MSE is about $4.6\times10^{-14}$, yet one-step execution crosses the rollout failure threshold.

\subsection{How can a too-short history fit perfectly?}
With $r=3<h=4$, the observations alone cannot recover the state: $\rank\mathcal O_3=8<10$. Yet the regression fits the expert almost exactly. The reason is in $S_r$. Its rows include the past \emph{expert actions} $K^*F^jx_s$, and these are functions of the full state. In this case $\rank S_3=10=n$: observations and expert actions together determine the hidden state on expert data. The regression learns to read the missing information from the action inputs.

At deployment those inputs are the learner's own actions. They no longer encode the expert's view of the state, so the learned map can feed its own outputs back as if they were state information. This is precisely the off-expert behavior that demonstrations leave undetermined. With $r=h$, the observations already recover the state, and this leakage is not needed.

\textbf{What this tells us.} The kernel condition of Section 3 applies to arbitrary inputs. On expert data the relevant identifiability condition is weaker, $\rank S_r=n$. The gap between the two conditions is where a policy can validate perfectly yet fail in closed loop.

\subsection{Why the one-step baseline must use the same windows}
If one method trains on different times or more examples, we cannot cleanly attribute a difference to chunk execution. We therefore match the input windows and fit the one-step target separately.
\begin{lemma}[Chunk training does not improve the first row in this regression]
With the same windows, first-action targets, and unnormalized ridge penalty $\lambda>0$, the first block of the chunk fit equals the separately fitted one-step policy.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The squared loss and squared Frobenius penalty are sums over output blocks. The optimization problem for block zero is identical in both fits and has a unique minimizer. Later target blocks do not enter that optimization.
\end{proof}
Thus $\ell=1$ produces the same controller in both cases. Differences at larger $\ell$ concern execution. This argument does not transfer automatically to a neural network whose outputs share learned parameters. Also, overlapping windows are correlated; 768 windows do not mean 768 independent trajectories.

\section{Why fresh feedback helps when the plan is otherwise correct}
Imagine making an exact plan at time $b$, then receiving an unexpected push $w_b$. At the next step, the actual state differs from the planned state by $w_b$. A full-state expert would change its action by $K^*w_b$. A committed chunk cannot make that correction yet.

More generally, define the action discrepancy at the learner's state by $\delta_t=u_t-K^*x_t$. This compares the chosen action with what the expert would do \emph{here}, not what it does on a separate reference trajectory.
\begin{proposition}[Disturbances make a nominal chunk outdated]
Suppose the boundary state is known exactly and $u_{b+j}=K^*F^jx_b$. Then
\[
\delta_{b+j}=-K^*\sum_{i=0}^{j-1}A^{j-1-i}w_{b+i}.
\]
For independent zero-mean disturbances with covariance $Q$,
\[
\E\norm{\delta_{b+j}}^2=
\sum_{q=0}^{j-1}\tr\bigl(K^*A^qQ(A^q)^\top(K^*)^\top\bigr).
\]
This expected discrepancy is nondecreasing in the phase $j$ within the chunk.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $d_j=x_{b+j}-F^jx_b$. The committed actions match the nominal expert actions, so subtraction gives $d_{j+1}=Ad_j+w_{b+j}$ with $d_0=0$. Unroll this recursion and use $\delta_{b+j}=-K^*d_j$. Independence removes cross terms in the squared norm; every remaining trace is nonnegative.
\end{proof}

The appearance of $A$, rather than $F$, matters: deviations are evolving without corrective feedback during the chunk. This explains a cost of commitment. It does \emph{not} prove that every learned controller improves when replanning more often. An inaccurate history-to-action map can also make harmful corrections more often.

To connect actions with the plotted state error, let $x_t^*$ denote a separate expert trajectory receiving the same disturbances, and set $e_t=x_t-x_t^*$.
\begin{lemma}[Stable expert dynamics filter action errors]
The tracking error obeys $e_{t+1}=Fe_t+B\delta_t$. If $\norm{F}\le\gamma<1$ and $\norm{\delta_t}\le d$, then
\[
\norm{e_t}\le\gamma^t\norm{e_0}+\norm{B}d\frac{1-\gamma^t}{1-\gamma}.
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Subtract the two plant updates. Their identical disturbances cancel, and substituting $u_t=K^*x_t+\delta_t$ gives the recursion. Iterate the norm inequality and sum the geometric series.
\end{proof}
The premise concerns action errors \emph{during deployment}. Validation loss does not supply the bound $d$. A stable $F$ alone cannot ensure that the learner is stable because $\delta_t$ can grow with the learner's state.

\section{To decide stability, include the controller's memory}
The next action depends on the past. Therefore the physical state alone is not enough to describe the learned feedback dynamics. We enlarge it to include the memory:
\[
s_t=\operatorname{col}(x_t,y_{t-r+1},\ldots,y_{t-1},u_{t-r+1},\ldots,u_{t-1}).
\]
The current observation is omitted from storage because it is already $Cx_t$. Updating $s_t$ means advancing the plant, dropping the oldest entries, and appending the current observation and action. All these operations are linear:
\[
s_{t+1}=Ds_t+Eu_t+Jw_t,\qquad z_t=Ps_t,\qquad x_t=Xs_t.
\]
$D$ advances the plant and shifts old entries; $E$ inserts the action's effects; $J$ inserts the disturbance into the plant state; $P$ assembles the policy input; $X$ selects the physical state. These matrices depend on the plant and $r$, not on a new learning procedure.

For $r=2$ with scalar observation and action, this is especially concrete:
\[
s_t=\begin{bmatrix}x_t\\y_{t-1}\\u_{t-1}\end{bmatrix},\quad
D=\begin{bmatrix}A&0&0\\C&0&0\\0&0&0\end{bmatrix},\quad
E=\begin{bmatrix}B\\0\\1\end{bmatrix},\quad
J=\begin{bmatrix}I\\0\\0\end{bmatrix},\quad
P=\begin{bmatrix}0&1&0\\C&0&0\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix}.
\]
The first block of $s_t$ has $n$ entries. Multiplication by $P$ returns $(y_{t-1},y_t,u_{t-1})$ in exactly the regression feature order.

\subsection{Look at the system only when it replans}
For $\ell=2$, the first update uses $W_0Ps_b$ and the second uses $W_1Ps_b$. Ignoring noise for a moment,
\[
s_{b+1}=(D+EW_0P)s_b,\qquad
s_{b+2}=(D^2+DEW_0P+EW_1P)s_b.
\]
Both actions use the original boundary feature. This is why the last term is $EW_1P$, rather than a new feedback multiplication using $s_{b+1}$.

\begin{proposition}[One matrix describes an entire execution interval]\label{prop:boundary}
For any fixed fitted policy and $\ell$, set
\[
\mathcal M_{r,\ell}=D^\ell+\sum_{j=0}^{\ell-1}D^{\ell-1-j}EW_jP,
\qquad
\mathcal N_{r,\ell}=[D^{\ell-1}J\ \cdots\ J].
\]
With $b=k\ell$ and $\omega_k=\operatorname{col}(w_b,\ldots,w_{b+\ell-1})$,
\[
\boxed{s_{(k+1)\ell}=\mathcal M_{r,\ell}s_{k\ell}+\mathcal N_{r,\ell}\omega_k.}
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
At each phase insert $u_{b+j}=W_jPs_b$ into the buffer update. A contribution injected at phase $j$ is propagated by $D$ for the remaining $\ell-1-j$ steps. Summing the propagated state, action, and noise contributions gives the formula.
\end{proof}

\textbf{What this tells us.} Changing history changes both the fitted policy and its memory. Changing execution changes how the fitted output blocks combine into feedback. There is no reason these matrix changes must improve stability monotonically.

\begin{theorem}[The stability test]
For fixed finite $r,\ell$, the noiseless augmented system is exponentially stable for all augmented initial conditions exactly when
\[
\rho(\mathcal M_{r,\ell})<1,
\]
where $\rho$ is the largest eigenvalue magnitude.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
At boundaries, $s_{k\ell}=\mathcal M_{r,\ell}^ks_0$. Powers of a finite matrix decay exponentially exactly when its eigenvalues lie strictly inside the unit circle. Between boundaries there are finitely many fixed linear maps from $s_{k\ell}$ to each phase, so exponential decay holds there too.
\end{proof}

Think first of the scalar recursion $s_{k+1}=as_k$: $a=0.8$ shrinks perturbations and $a=1.1$ amplifies them. Eigenvalues generalize this idea to multiple directions. The rate $\rho(\mathcal M)^{1/\ell}$ expresses decay per plant step, which is more comparable across different $\ell$. Eigenvalues do not describe all transient amplification.

This is a full augmented-state test. Physically constrained initial histories may avoid some directions; noise can excite others. A growing mode matters only if it is excited. A finite successful rollout does not establish stability, and the simulator's failure cutoff is not itself an eigenvalue calculation.

\section{Even a stable policy can be sensitive to noise}
Stability asks whether an initial perturbation eventually disappears without new disturbances. Sensitivity asks how much variation remains when disturbances keep arriving. These are different questions.

For the scalar recursion $s_{k+1}=as_k+\xi_k$, suppose $|a|<1$ and the independent disturbances have variance $q$. At steady state, old variance is multiplied by $a^2$ and fresh variance $q$ is added:
\[
S=a^2S+q\quad\Longrightarrow\quad S=\frac{q}{1-a^2}.
\]
Both $a=0.5$ and $a=0.99$ are stable, but the latter retains much more disturbance energy. Matrix covariance is the same bookkeeping with correlations included.

\begin{proposition}[Steady-state boundary covariance]
Write $M=\mathcal M_{r,\ell}$ and $N=\mathcal N_{r,\ell}$. Suppose $M$ is stable, $w_t$ are independent and zero-mean with covariance $Q$, and the initial condition has finite second moment and is independent of future noise. The limiting boundary covariance solves
\[
S=MSM^\top+N(I_\ell\otimes Q)N^\top.
\]
Here $I_\ell\otimes Q$ is simply a block diagonal matrix containing $\ell$ copies of $Q$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Take covariance in the boundary update. Independence removes cross terms. Repeated substitution gives the convergent sum
$S=\sum_{k\ge0}M^kN(I_\ell\otimes Q)N^\top(M^k)^\top$;
stability makes the initial covariance vanish and gives uniqueness.
\end{proof}

Our plots measure distance from a \emph{separate expert}, not distance from zero. We must therefore track learner and expert jointly. Because they receive the same noise, their errors are correlated: subtracting their trajectories is not equivalent to adding their variances. Appendix~\ref{app:covariance} gives the exact calculation. It also averages over every position within a chunk, not just the replanning times.

\section{What the results are saying in this language}
The November 1 saved run used $H=16$, $\ell\in\{1,2,4,8,16\}$, 600 scored steps, and the final 200 steps for tail error. There were three seeds and eight evaluation trajectories per seed. Training was noiseless; evaluation used process noise with standard deviation $0.02$ per state coordinate.

There are two distinct observations. When $r=h$, one-step execution has the lowest mean tail error in 19 of 20 configurations. The exception has only a $0.12\%$ advantage for $\ell=2$, inconsistently across seeds. This fits the picture of fresh feedback correcting disturbances.

With shorter history, frequent replanning can instead perform poorly. For $n=10,h=4,r=3$, execution lengths $1,4,8$ cross the numerical failure threshold, while $\ell=2$ has tail error $2.80\times10^{-4}$ and $\ell=16$ has $3.61\times10^{-3}$. With $r=4$, one-step execution has much lower error, $7.64\times10^{-6}$. Chunking helps one poorly informed fitted controller, but supplying more history helps more in this example.

The proposed explanation is that history and execution jointly change the learned feedback matrix. We checked this by constructing $\mathcal M_{r,\ell}$ from the fitted weights (\texttt{boundary\_matrix} in \texttt{long\_experiment.py}). For seed 618:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{c|cc|cc}
&\multicolumn{2}{c|}{$r=3$}&\multicolumn{2}{c}{$r=4$}\\
$\ell$&$\rho(\mathcal M)$&rollout&$\rho(\mathcal M)$&rollout\\\hline
1&1.32&failed&0.992&stable\\
2&0.985&stable&0.985&stable\\
4&2.35&failed&0.970&stable\\
8&2.99&failed&0.940&stable\\
16&0.884&stable&0.884&stable
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Across all three seeds and contexts $r\in\{1,3,4,5\}$ for $(n,h)=(10,4)$, $\rho(\mathcal M_{r,\ell})\ge1$ coincides exactly with rollout failure (60 of 60 chunk configurations). Over the full sweep (1095 chunk configurations), every rollout failure has $\rho(\mathcal M)\ge1$; 9 further configurations have $\rho(\mathcal M)>1$ and growing error but had not reached the failure cutoff within 600 steps. All unstable configurations have $r<h$. With $r=4$, the per-step rate $\rho^{1/\ell}=0.9923$ for every $\ell$, equal to the expert's own rate $\rho(F)$: once the history suffices, the learned loop reproduces the expert loop, and only disturbance handling distinguishes execution lengths. With $r=3$ the stable cases are not monotone in $\ell$, which matches the claim that no monotone relationship should be expected. The covariance prediction of Appendix~\ref{app:covariance} has not yet been compared with simulated tail error.

The expert controls the first $r_{\max}-1$ steps of every evaluation episode (a warm-up that fills the history buffer); replanning and scoring begin afterward. The expert system is only weakly stable, $\rho(F)\approx0.992$, so small feedback errors can move the learned loop across the unit circle.

Changing $h$ also changes sensors. Hold $(n,h)$ fixed to isolate history and execution effects. The final 200 steps of a finite run are not automatically a stationary measurement, and three seeds on fixed plants do not establish a universal law.

\section{How can this story continue for nonlinear dynamics?}
The linear story has three parts: history estimates the state, the policy predicts actions, and feedback determines whether mistakes shrink or grow. Those same questions remain meaningful when
\[
x_{t+1}=f(x_t,u_t)+w_t,\qquad y_t=g(x_t),\qquad u_t^*=\pi^*(x_t).
\]
What changes is that the answers are no longer given by fixed matrices everywhere.

\subsection{First replace exact reconstruction with an error budget}
Suppose an estimator $\phi_r$ turns history into a state estimate with error at most $\varepsilon_r$ \emph{on the histories encountered in deployment}. This is an assumption to justify, not a consequence of using a long window.

Let $\Phi(x)=f(x,\pi^*(x))$ be the expert's noiseless state update, and let $G_j(x)=\pi^*(\Phi^j(x))$ be its action $j$ steps into the future. Suppose $G_j$ changes by at most $L_j$ times a change in state. If the learned output has fitting error at most $\eta_{r,j}$ relative to $G_j(\phi_r(z))$, then
\[
\norm{\widehat G_{r,j}(z)-G_j(x)}
\le \underbrace{\eta_{r,j}}_{\text{prediction error}}
+\underbrace{L_j\varepsilon_r}_{\text{state uncertainty propagated to action}}.
\]
The proof is to add and subtract $G_j(\phi_r(z))$ and apply the triangle inequality. This decomposition explains what a useful learning theorem would have to control.

\begin{proposition}[A nonlinear error bound for one chunk]
Assume $\norm{f(x,u)-f(x',u')}\le L_x\norm{x-x'}+L_u\norm{u-u'}$ and the preceding estimation/prediction bounds hold on the relevant region. Compare the learner with the nominal expert started at the same boundary state, $\bar x_j=\Phi^j(x_b)$. Then
\[
\norm{x_{b+j}-\bar x_j}\le
\sum_{i=0}^{j-1}L_x^{j-1-i}
\left[L_u(\eta_{r,i}+L_i\varepsilon_r)+\norm{w_{b+i}}\right].
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The discrepancy starts at zero. At each step its previous value is multiplied by at most $L_x$, while action error contributes at most $L_u(\eta_{r,j}+L_j\varepsilon_r)$ and the disturbance contributes $\norm{w_{b+j}}$. Iterating this scalar inequality gives the sum.
\end{proof}

\textbf{How to read the bound.} Each step injects a new error, and the powers of $L_x$ describe how earlier errors are amplified before replanning. This does not yet compare with one fixed expert trajectory over many chunks: the reference here restarts at the learner's current state. A separate stability argument must connect boundaries.

\subsection{Then examine the nonlinear replanning map}
Include the physical state and history in $s$ as before. Let $\mathscr D(s,u)$ update the plant and buffers, and let $\mathscr P(s)$ form the history feature. Define
\[
\Psi_0(s)=s,\qquad
\Psi_{j+1}(s)=\mathscr D\bigl(\Psi_j(s),\widehat\pi_{r,j}(\mathscr P(s))\bigr).
\]
The map $\Psi_{r,\ell}=\Psi_\ell$ advances one entire chunk. Notice that the predicted actions still use the original boundary history. Near an equilibrium, a smooth curved map is approximated by its tangent matrix, the Jacobian $\mathcal A=D\Psi_{r,\ell}(0)$.

\begin{theorem}[A local nonlinear baseline]
Suppose the origin is an equilibrium of the noiseless boundary map, the maps are continuously differentiable nearby, and $\rho(\mathcal A)<1$. Then the equilibrium is locally exponentially stable.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For a stable matrix $\mathcal A$, choose a positive-definite matrix $P$ defining $\norm{s}_P=\sqrt{s^\top Ps}$ with $\norm{\mathcal A}_P<1$. Differentiability gives $\Psi(s)=\mathcal As+o(\norm{s}_P)$. On a sufficiently small ball, the remainder is small enough that $\norm{\Psi(s)}_P\le q\norm{s}_P$ for some $q<1$. The ball is invariant and each boundary update contracts. Smooth within-chunk maps preserve local exponential convergence.
\end{proof}

This says that a sufficiently small error shrinks near a particular operating point. It says nothing by itself about large errors or arbitrary initial conditions. If the fitted policy does not preserve the proposed equilibrium, we must first find the actual equilibrium. A Jacobian with spectral radius one makes this test inconclusive.

At a common equilibrium, let $D,E$ be the state/input derivatives of $\mathscr D$, $P$ the derivative of $\mathscr P$, and $W_j$ the derivative of the $j$th policy output. Differentiating the recursion above gives
\[
\mathcal A=D^\ell+\sum_{j=0}^{\ell-1}D^{\ell-1-j}EW_jP.
\]
This is precisely the linear boundary formula, now applied to local derivatives. The linear analysis therefore becomes the first approximation to the nonlinear problem, rather than an unrelated preliminary exercise.

\subsection{What about ongoing disturbances?}
A useful local assumption is
$\norm{\Psi(s)}_P\le q\norm{s}_P$ on a ball of radius $R$, with $q<1$. If each disturbed boundary update differs from this nominal update by at most $d$, and $d\le(1-q)R$, then
\[
\norm{s_k}_P\le q^k\norm{s_0}_P+\frac{1-q^k}{1-q}d\le R
\quad\text{when }\norm{s_0}_P\le R.
\]
Indeed, the one-step inequality is $\norm{s_{k+1}}_P\le q\norm{s_k}_P+d$; the condition on $d$ keeps the ball invariant, and summing the recursion proves the bound. The ratio $d/(1-q)$ has a direct interpretation: disturbance size divided by the strength of recovery.

This is a bounded-disturbance result. Gaussian noise can produce arbitrarily large disturbances, so an all-time local guarantee does not automatically apply to the Gaussian simulations. Nor does the linear covariance equation become an exact nonlinear formula: it is only a small-noise approximation unless additional conditions are proved.

\section{A manageable next step for the project}
First use the fitted linear policies to test the exact boundary matrix and stationary tracking predictions. This should explain particular failures before we seek a general statistical theorem. Then introduce a small nonlinear perturbation,
\[
x_{t+1}=Ax_t+Bu_t+\alpha\tanh(Vx_t)+w_t,
\]
retaining $K^*x$ as the expert initially. Since componentwise $\tanh$ is 1-Lipschitz, the condition $\norm{A+BK^*}+\alpha\norm{V}<1$ ensures that the expert's noiseless update is contractive. It gives a controlled starting point, although it can restrict $\alpha$ severely for a weakly contractive linear system.

History reconstruction must be checked again: a nonlinear observation-history Jacobian with full column rank gives a local inverse on its image, not global uniqueness or exact recovery under process noise. Compare a linear history policy with a smooth nonlinear policy to distinguish representational limitations from feedback effects.

The claim to investigate is now precise: \emph{history affects what the policy can infer and what demonstrations identify; execution affects how that fitted policy feeds its decisions back into the dynamics.} The project seeks to predict the resulting stability and tracking error. It does not assume a universal monotone relationship between the two lengths.

\section{The exact tracking-covariance calculation}\label{app:covariance}
This appendix is a reference for implementation. Its purpose is to turn the intuitive variance bookkeeping into the same per-coordinate tracking metric used by the experiments.

Use $M=\mathcal M_{r,\ell}$, $N=\mathcal N_{r,\ell}$ and the buffer matrices from Section 7. Define phase maps $T_0=I$, $T_{j+1}=DT_j+EW_jP$. Then
\[
s_{b+j}=T_js_b+\sum_{i=0}^{j-1}D^{j-1-i}Jw_{b+i}.
\]
At boundaries, jointly store learner and expert as $v_k=\operatorname{col}(s_{k\ell},x^*_{k\ell})$. The joint dynamics use
\[
\mathcal A=\diag(M,F^\ell),\qquad
N_*=[F^{\ell-1}\ \cdots\ I],\qquad
\mathcal B=\begin{bmatrix}N\\N_*\end{bmatrix}.
\]
Assume $M,F$ are stable and the noise/initial conditions satisfy the covariance assumptions above. The limiting covariance is the unique solution
\[
\Sigma=\mathcal A\Sigma\mathcal A^\top+
\mathcal B(I_\ell\otimes Q)\mathcal B^\top.
\]
This includes the correlation between learner and expert caused by shared disturbances.

At phase $j$, define $L_j=[XT_j\ \ -F^j]$ and
\[
R_j=[XD^{j-1}J-F^{j-1}\ \cdots\ XJ-I],
\]
where $R_0$ has no columns. The tracking error is the boundary contribution $L_jv_k$ plus the within-chunk noise contribution $R_j\operatorname{col}(w_b,\ldots,w_{b+j-1})$.
\begin{proposition}[Long-run expected tracking error]
The phase-averaged, per-coordinate expected squared tracking error is
\[
\mathcal J(r,\ell)=\frac{1}{n\ell}\sum_{j=0}^{\ell-1}
\tr\left(L_j\Sigma L_j^\top+R_j(I_j\otimes Q)R_j^\top\right),
\]
with zero second term for $j=0$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The boundary state is independent of future disturbances, so cross terms between the two contributions vanish in covariance. Add their covariances, take the trace to obtain expected squared norm, divide by $n$, and average over the $\ell$ phases. Stability makes initial transients vanish in the long-run average of expected errors.
\end{proof}
This is an expectation statement. Almost-sure convergence of an empirical average requires the corresponding ergodicity assumptions. A short tail window can differ because of transients, random variation, or unequal counts of the different chunk phases.
